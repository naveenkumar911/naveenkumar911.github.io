Forward deployed engineering is now the highest-paid generalist role in AI. Frontier labs and applied-AI companies pay a premium for people who can embed with a customer, ship a production AI system in weeks, and leave behind measurable outcomes. The individual version of the role is well documented. The leadership version is less discussed and is where the leverage, and the compensation, concentrate.

I have done both: ten years embedded with clients across government, retail, asset management, pharma and sports media, and now leading a team that runs many such engagements at once. This is what changes.

## The individual FDE

An individual forward deployed engineer owns one engagement: discovery, scoping, building the first version, hardening, cutover, handover. Success is a metric on the customer's P&L and a customer team that can run the system. The skills are technical depth, customer empathy, scoping discipline and the willingness to write production code in someone else's environment.

## The FDE leader

The leader owns the gate sequence across a portfolio of engagements simultaneously, and four things that no individual engagement produces.

**A repeatable method.** Discovery templates, success-criteria formats, golden-set practices, readiness checklists, handover standards. My [six-gate playbook](/architecture/forward-deployed-playbook) exists because I needed twelve programs to run the same way without me in every room.

**A team that can run it.** Hiring people who combine engineering depth with customer judgment is hard; the pool is thin. Onboarding them into the method, giving them ownership of engagements, and coaching them through the first difficult customer conversation is the core management work. I hired 8 and onboarded 12 engineers into this way of working in the last eighteen months.

**Escalation and portfolio judgment.** When a gate is at risk (no owner, baseline disputed, security blocked, cutover slipping), the leader is the escalation point. When six programs run concurrently, the leader decides where the scarce senior attention goes this week, and which program should stop.

**The field-to-product loop.** Individual FDEs learn what customers need. Leaders turn that into product: reference architectures, platform capabilities, tooling, and specific feedback to model and product teams. This is the loop that frontier labs are paying for; it is why the role sits at the revenue edge and the product edge at once.

## Why the leverage is here

Three structural reasons.

First, enterprise adoption is gated by deployment capacity, not by model capability. Every lab and every applied-AI company has more demand than people who can convert it into production systems. The leader who can build and run a deployment organisation multiplies that capacity.

Second, the method is the moat. Models converge; the ability to reliably get them into a regulated enterprise, with governance, in weeks, does not. A leader with a proven method and a team trained in it is a compounding asset.

Third, the field-to-product loop is how AI companies learn what to build next. Leaders who close it well shape the roadmap.

## What I look for when hiring into the role

- Has shipped, not demoed, at least one AI system into a customer's production environment.
- Can describe the baseline, the metric and what happened after cutover.
- Has had a difficult conversation with a customer about scope and can explain how it ended.
- Writes production code and is honest about what they would not build.
- Treats evaluation and handover as part of the build.

## What I would tell a lab or company hiring an FDE leader

Look for the method, the team-building record and the loop, not just the engagement war stories. Ask how many programs they have run concurrently and what they stopped. Ask for the playbook. The individuals who ship are valuable; the leaders who make shipping repeatable are the ones who change your deployment capacity.
