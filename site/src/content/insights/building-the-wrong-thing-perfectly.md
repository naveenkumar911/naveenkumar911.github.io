The biggest AI failure in 2026 may not be hallucination. It may be building the wrong thing perfectly.

Companies are moving fast: new copilots, new agents, new AI platforms, new automation projects every quarter. Speed without direction creates something dangerous: AI-powered inefficiency. If a broken process becomes automated, the business has not been transformed. The broken process is just faster.

## Two starting points

The common path looks like this:

New model → impressive demo → find a use case.

The path that produces value looks like this:

Business problem → workflow → data → AI capability → governance → measurable outcome.

The order matters. Starting from the model anchors every later decision on what the model can do rather than on what the business needs to change.

## Uncomfortable questions before approval

The strongest AI leaders I know ask these before approving another initiative:

- What business metric will this improve, and what is its baseline today?
- What workflow are we actually changing? Draw it, before and after.
- What data does the AI need, and does it exist at the quality required?
- What happens when the AI is wrong? Who notices, how fast, and what does recovery cost?
- Where does human accountability remain?
- Can we measure ROI after deployment, not just estimate it before?
- Can this scale beyond the pilot, or does it depend on conditions that only exist in the pilot?

If two or more of these have no answer, the initiative is a research project, which is fine as long as it is funded and labelled as one.

## A concrete example

At a retailer I embedded with, the first request was a chatbot for store staff. The workshop showed that the real cost was not staff asking questions; it was support agents re-answering the same questions across channels with inconsistent answers, and stores waiting hours for confirmations. The workflow change was consistent, cited answers delivered where the question arose, plus automation of the confirmation steps that did not need a human at all.

The system we built was still, technically, a chatbot with retrieval and agents. But it was scoped to a metric (resolution time), a workflow (support triage and confirmation), and data we validated first. It delivered $2M a year in savings. The originally requested chatbot would have been a nicer FAQ.

## The role of governance in "building the right thing"

Governance is usually framed as a brake. Used properly it is the steering. A use-case register that requires an owner, a baseline and a success criterion filters out the wrong things before engineering starts. Blast-radius classes force the conversation about where humans remain accountable. A release gate on cost and quality prevents a beautiful solution from shipping to a problem that did not need it.

## The future belongs to repeatable conversion

The future does not belong to organisations with the most AI experiments. It belongs to organisations that can repeatedly turn AI capability into business advantage. That requires technology, and also strategy, governance, operating-model design and leadership.

AI leadership is no longer about understanding what AI can do. It is about deciding what the business should do with it.
